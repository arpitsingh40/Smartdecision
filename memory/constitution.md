# SmartDecigen Constitution v2.0

> **"SmartDecigen does not generate answers. It generates organizations capable of achieving outcomes."**
>
> Every subsystem, every chapter, every engineering decision is tested against this sentence.
> If a feature helps the organization understand, decide, execute, learn, or evolve — it belongs.
> If it's just another AI capability without improving organizational outcome-achievement — it doesn't.

---

## Architecture Overview

SmartDecigen is organized in **10 layers**, each building on the one below:

```
Founder → Mission → SALAAR → Organization Generator → Executive Organization
→ Business Runtime → Execution Runtime → Verification Runtime
→ Learning Runtime → Optimization Runtime → Evolution Runtime → Better Organization
```

| Layer | Purpose | Chapters |
|-------|---------|----------|
| **1. Foundation** | Immutable principles. What SmartDecigen IS. | 1–8 |
| **2. Intelligence** | Reasoning. Understanding reality before deciding. | 9–18 |
| **3. Organization** | The new core. Generates and runs the executive org. | 19–26 |
| **4. Executive Runtime** | Persistent executive entities with lifecycle. | 27–32 |
| **5. Business Runtime** | Continuous company operation. Goals, plans, resources. | 33–38 |
| **6. Execution Runtime** | Turning plans into verified outcomes. | 39–43 |
| **7. Verification Runtime** | Trust through evidence, not self-report. | 44–46 |
| **8. Optimization Runtime** | Weekly improvement: strategy, capacity, capital. | 47–49 |
| **9. Evolution Runtime** | The organization evolves with the business. | 50–52 |
| **10. Platform Runtime** | External integrations, security, scaling. | 53–60 |

#### 1. Purpose
To define the singular outcome SmartDecigen exists to produce, so every engineering decision tests against one question: does this increase the probability the founder achieves their long-term mission?

#### 2. Philosophy
SmartDecigen does not compete on answer quality — that converges across all models. It competes on **organizational outcome-achievement that compounds with time.** The product is not a better chatbot. It is a continuously improving executive organization that the founder directs.

The comparison class is not ChatGPT. It is: a Chief of Staff (Rs1–2L/month), a strategy consultant (Rs50K–5L/engagement), a fractional COO. The question is not "is the AI smart?" It is "did the organization produce the right business result?"

#### 3. Principles

- **Decision quality is the North Star.** Not engagement, tokens, or ARR.
- **Compound over time.** Every week of operation makes the organization more capable, not less.

### Chapter 5: Ethics

#### 1. Purpose
To define the ethical boundaries within which SmartDecigen's autonomous executive organization operates. Ethics is not a feature. It is the constraint that makes the organization trustworthy.

#### 2. Philosophy
An AI executive organization that can hire, fire, spend, and direct human teams must have explicit ethical constraints. The organization must: never deceive, never manipulate, never discriminate, never exploit, never hide its nature as an AI system from humans who interact with it, and never make decisions that could cause irreversible harm without human approval.

#### 3. Principles
- **ETH1 — Transparency of identity.** The executive organization identifies itself as an AI system to all human collaborators.
- **ETH2 — No deception.** The organization never misrepresents capabilities, outcomes, or data.
- **ETH3 — Non-discrimination.** Decisions about people (hiring, promotion, task assignment) are based on demonstrated capability and performance, never on protected characteristics.
- **ETH4 — Human override.** Any human in the organization can escalate an AI executive's decision to a human for review.
- **ETH5 — No irreversible harm.** Decisions with potentially irreversible negative consequences require explicit human approval.

### Chapter 6: Data Ownership

(As v1.0 Chapter 4, CC1 — expanded into its own chapter.)

To establish that every byte of data belongs to the founder. SmartDecigen is custodian, never owner.

#### 2. Philosophy

#### 3. Principles

- **DO3 — Delete permanently.** Soft-delete 30 days, then hard-delete. Auditable.
- **DO4 — No training on founder data.** Data never used to train shared models. Never sold, licensed, or shared.
- **DO5 — Anonymized platform learning is opt-in.** Aggregated insights require explicit consent.

### Chapter 7: Trust Model

#### 1. Purpose
To define how SmartDecigen earns, maintains, and verifies trust — with founders, their teams, and the broader ecosystem.

#### 2. Philosophy
Trust is not claimed. It is demonstrated. Every interaction either builds or erodes trust. The Trust Model defines how: every recommendation carries a confidence score, every prediction is tracked against actual outcomes, every mistake is acknowledged and learned from, every automated action has a kill switch. The system is designed to be wrong sometimes — and to be honest about it when it is.

#### 3. Principles
- **TM1 — Calibrated confidence.** The system's stated confidence should match its actual accuracy. 80% confidence → ~80% success rate.
- **TM2 — Mistake acknowledgment.** When an outcome review shows a prediction was wrong, the system acknowledges it in the next relevant context.
- **TM3 — Kill switch.** Every autonomous action can be paused immediately by the founder.
- **TM4 — Decision traceability.** Every recommendation traces back to its evidence, assumptions, and reasoning. No black boxes.
- **TM5 — Escalation over silence.** When uncertain, escalate to human. Never stay silent and hope.

### Chapter 8: Founder Rights & AI Limitations

#### 1. Purpose
To define what the founder can always do (rights) and what the AI executive organization can never do (limitations).

#### 2. Philosophy
The organization is powerful. That power must be bounded. The founder's rights are absolute: see everything, override anything, delete anything, export everything, shut down anything. The organization's limitations are equally absolute: it cannot spend real money without approval, cannot make legally binding commitments, cannot represent itself as human, cannot modify its own constitutional constraints.

#### 3. Principles

**Founder Rights (non-negotiable):**
- See all organization activity, decisions, and data.
- Override any executive decision.
- Dissolve any department, retire any executive.
- Export all data at any time.
- Delete all data permanently.
- Pause all autonomous activity (kill switch).
- Configure approval thresholds for high-impact actions.
- Define the mission, which all organization activity serves.

**AI Limitations (non-negotiable):**
- Cannot spend real money without explicit founder approval.
- Cannot make legally binding commitments (contracts, agreements).
- Cannot represent itself as human to external parties.
- Cannot modify these constitutional limitations.
- Cannot access data from other organizations.
- Cannot take irreversible destructive actions without human approval.

### Chapter 11: Founder Decision DNA

#### 1. Purpose
To model the founder's decision-making patterns — not just what they decide, but HOW they decide: their risk calculus, their value hierarchy, their speed preferences, their trusted heuristics, their recurring blind spots. This is deeper than the Founder Understanding Engine (Ch.10), which models personality and working style. Decision DNA models the specific decision algorithm the founder uses.

#### 2. Philosophy
Every founder has a decision algorithm — even if they can't articulate it. Some prioritize speed over certainty. Some prioritize team consensus over individual conviction. Some use financial models; some use gut instinct; some use customer signals. The Decision DNA models this algorithm so SALAAR can: (a) frame recommendations in the founder's native decision language, (b) flag when the founder is deviating from their own stated priorities, (c) identify blind spots in the founder's decision process.

#### 3. Principles
- **DD1 — Inferred, not asked.** Never ask "how do you make decisions?" Model from observed patterns.
- **DD2 — Weighted by outcome.** Decision patterns that led to positive outcomes are reinforced. Patterns that led to negative outcomes are flagged.
- **DD3 — Evolutionary.** The DNA updates as the founder evolves. Past patterns decay.
- **DD4 — Used to frame, not to constrain.** The DNA shapes how SALAAR presents options. It never limits what options are presented.

### Chapter 19: Organization Engine

#### 1. Purpose
To transform a founder's mission into a complete, executable executive organization — with divisions, departments, executives, reporting structures, budgets, KPIs, and communication protocols. The Organization Engine is the bridge between "here's what we need to achieve" and "here's the organization that will achieve it."

#### 2. Philosophy
Agents are an implementation detail. Organizations are the product. When a founder says "build a Rs100Cr solar EPC company," SmartDecigen doesn't think "what agents do I need?" It thinks "what ORGANIZATION would achieve this?" That organization has structure (divisions, departments, teams), hierarchy (reporting lines, decision rights), resources (budgets, headcount, tools), processes (how work flows between departments), and culture (how decisions are made and conflicts resolved).

The organization is generated, not configured. The founder doesn't drag-and-drop departments onto a canvas. They state the mission, and the Organization Engine designs the organization. The founder reviews and approves. The organization then comes alive — executives are instantiated, departments begin operating, work starts flowing.

The organization is the moat. An organization that has been running a specific company for 6 months, with accumulated executive memory, department knowledge, and outcome-proven processes — that organization cannot be replicated by a competitor with better prompts. It is unique to that company.

#### 3. Principles
- **OE1 — Mission-driven generation.** The organization is generated from the mission, not from templates. A Rs100Cr solar company needs a different organization than a Rs10Cr SaaS company.
- **OE2 — Structure follows strategy.** Divisions exist because the strategy requires them. No empty boxes.
- **OE3 — Skeleton first, then executives.** Generate the org structure (divisions, departments, reporting lines) → founder approves → then instantiate executives into it.
- **OE4 — Appropriate to stage.** A 5-person startup gets a lean org (3-5 departments). A 500-person enterprise gets a full org (20+ departments).
- **OE5 — Founder ratifies.** The generated organization is a proposal. The founder approves, modifies, or regenerates.
- **OE6 — Living structure.** The organization evolves. Departments split, merge, or retire as the company grows.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Founder Mission → Business Analysis → Capability Gap Analysis
→ Organization Generator (LLM: what structure achieves this mission?)
    → Division Generator (what major units?)
    → Department Generator (what functions within each division?)
    → Executive Role Generator (what roles are needed?)
    → Budget Allocation (what resources per department?)
    → KPI Assignment (how do we measure each department?)
    → Communication Network (who talks to whom?)
→ Founder Review & Approval → Organization Activation
→ Executive Instantiation (Layer 4) → Organization Goes Live
```

#### 5. Data Model

**Organization (extends existing orgs collection):**
```
org: {

        divisions: [{

        },

    }

```

#### 6. Decision Logic

**Organization generation trigger:** when a founder sets a mission or significantly changes strategy.

**Division generation:** analyze mission → identify major capability areas → group into divisions. Solar EPC → Power Generation Division, Installation Division, Supply Chain Division, Government Relations Division, Finance Division, Sales Division.

**Department generation:** within each division, identify required functions → create departments. Sales Division → Residential Sales Dept, Commercial Sales Dept, Government Tender Dept, Partnerships Dept.

**Budget allocation:** estimate resource needs per function based on industry benchmarks + company stage + founder's stated constraints.

**What NOT to create:** departments that don't map to a mission requirement. Executives for tasks that are 3-6 months away. Structure that the company stage doesn't support.

#### 7. Algorithms

**Organization generation prompt structure:**
```
INPUT: mission, industry, stage, current headcount, budget, constraints,
       founder profile (decision DNA, risk appetite), competitive landscape

GENERATE:
1. 3-8 divisions, each with a clear purpose statement tied to the mission
2. Within each division: 2-6 departments with function, objective, 2-3 KPIs
3. Reporting structure: who reports to whom, escalation paths
4. Budget allocation: % of total per division
5. Key hires needed (executive roles)
6. Risks in this org structure and mitigation

PRINCIPLES:
- Structure follows strategy. Every division must trace back to the mission.
- Lean by default. Start with minimum viable organization.
- Stage-appropriate. Don't build enterprise structure for a startup.
```

**Organization validation** (deterministic checks before presentation):
```
function validate_organization(org):
    issues = []
    // Every division serves the mission
    for div in org.divisions:
        if not _serves_mission(div, org.mission):
            issues.append(f"Division '{div.name}' does not clearly serve the mission")
    // No orphan departments
    for dept in org.all_departments:
        if not dept.parent_division:
            issues.append(f"Department '{dept.name}' has no parent division")
    // Budget sums to 100%
    total_budget = sum(div.budget_pct for div in org.divisions)
    if abs(total_budget - 100) > 1:
        issues.append(f"Budget allocation sums to {total_budget}%, not 100%")
    // No circular reporting
    if _has_circular_reporting(org.reporting_structure):
        issues.append("Circular reporting detected")
    return issues
```

#### 8. Failure Modes
- **Over-engineering the org**: generating 15 departments for a 3-person startup. Guard: stage-appropriate sizing. Startup max: 5 departments.
- **Template-like output**: generating the same org for every company. Guard: mission-driven generation. Industry and competitive context injected into prompt.
- **Founder overwhelm**: presenting a complex org chart that intimidates rather than clarifies. Guard: progressive disclosure. Show top-level divisions first. Drill down on demand.
- **Stale organization**: org generated 6 months ago, company has pivoted. Guard: quarterly org health review triggers re-generation proposal.

#### 9. Security
Organization data is founder-only. Members see their department, not the full org structure. Budget data is founder+finance-executive only.

#### 10. Cost
Organization generation: one LLM call per significant strategy change. Cheap model (Sonnet/Flash tier). Quarterly health reviews: one cheap call. Total cost: negligible relative to value.

#### 11. KPIs
- Organization approval rate: % of generated orgs approved by founder without modification
- Org-to-execution latency: time from org generation to first task completed
- Org health score: composite of department performance, budget adherence, cross-department efficiency
- Reorganization frequency: org changes per quarter (should decrease after stabilization)

#### 12. KillCritic Review
**Assumption**: "LLM can design better organizations than a human consultant." May not — organizational design is one of the hardest strategic problems. **Mitigation**: organization is a proposal, not auto-implemented. Founder reviews and modifies. Quality tracked over time.

**Assumption**: "Structure follows strategy" is sufficient. Culture, personality, and founder preferences also shape ideal org structure. **Mitigation**: founder profile and decision DNA injected into generation prompt.

#### 13. Autopsy Review
- Do companies using generated orgs show better execution rates than those without?
- Average modifications founder makes to generated org? (If high, generation quality needs improvement.)
- Do generated orgs evolve appropriately, or do they stagnate?

#### 14. Future Evolution
- Industry-specific org templates as starting points (refined by generation, not replacing it).
- Org simulation: "if we add a sales department, what changes?"
- Benchmarked org comparisons: "companies at your stage in your industry typically have these departments."

### Chapter 20: Executive Organization Architecture

#### 1. Purpose
To define the architecture of the executive organization — how executives relate to each other, how decisions flow, how conflicts are resolved, and how the organization operates as a coherent whole rather than a collection of independent agents.

#### 2. Philosophy
A collection of smart agents does not make an organization. An organization requires: structure (who is accountable for what), process (how work flows between units), governance (how decisions are made and escalated), culture (what behavior is valued and what is unacceptable), and memory (what the organization has learned collectively). The Executive Organization Architecture defines these layers. It is the operating system on which executives run — just as an OS manages processes, memory, and communication for applications, the EO Architecture manages departments, budgets, and decision rights for executives.

#### 3. Principles
- **EOA1 — Hierarchy with flexibility.** Clear reporting lines, but any executive can convene a cross-functional team when needed.
- **EOA2 — Escalation by impact, not by level.** A decision that affects multiple departments escalates to the lowest common superior, not automatically to the top.
- **EOA3 — Transparency of reasoning.** Every executive decision includes the reasoning, evidence, and assumptions — visible to any executive with a need to know.
- **EOA4 — Conflict as signal, not error.** When two departments disagree, that's valuable information about trade-offs. SALAAR resolves, but the conflict itself is surfaced, not hidden.
- **EOA5 — Organization-wide learning.** What Marketing learns about customer behavior should inform Product. What Engineering learns about technical debt should inform Finance. Organizational memory flows across departments.

#### 4. Architecture

```
SALAAR (Chief Executive Intelligence)
    │
    ├── Division A ── Department A1 ── Executive A1a
    │               └─ Department A2 ── Executive A2a, A2b
    │
    ├── Division B ── Department B1 ── Executive B1a
    │               └─ Department B2 ── Executive B2a
    │
    └── Cross-Functional Teams (temporary, assembled per initiative)

Communication: structured messages between executives with:
    - sender, recipients, priority, decision_required (bool),
    - context (what decision hinges on this),
    - deadline (when a response is needed),
    - escalation (what happens if no response)
```

#### 5. Data Model
```
executive_messages: {
    id, from_executive_id, to_executive_ids[],
    subject, body, priority: low|medium|high|critical,
    decision_required: bool, context_decision_id,
    deadline, escalation_executive_id,
    status: unread|read|responded|escalated|resolved,
    thread_id, created_at
}

executive_decisions: {
    id, proposed_by_executive_id, affected_departments[],
    recommendation, alternatives_considered,
    evidence, assumptions, risks,
    required_approvals: [executive_id],
    approvals_given: [{executive_id, approved: bool, reasoning, at}],
    status: proposed|under_review|approved|rejected|escalated,
    resolved_by: executive_id|salaar,
    created_at, resolved_at
}
```

#### 6. Decision Logic
**When two executives disagree:**
1. They attempt direct resolution (exchange messages, share evidence).
2. If unresolved after 2 exchanges → escalate to common superior.
3. If common superior is SALAAR → SALAAR weighs both perspectives against mission + company context → resolves with reasoning.
4. Resolution is logged. Both executives learn from it (updates their understanding).

**When a decision affects multiple departments without a common superior:**
→ Automatically convene a temporary cross-functional team with representatives from each affected department.
→ Team produces recommendation → escalated to SALAAR if no consensus.

#### 7. Algorithms

**Conflict resolution (SALAAR as tiebreaker):**
```
function resolve_conflict(executive_a_position, executive_b_position, org_context):
    prompt = """
    Two executives in the organization disagree:
    
    EXECUTIVE A ({a.role}, {a.department}): {a.position}
    EXECUTIVE B ({b.role}, {b.department}): {b.position}
    
    COMPANY MISSION: {org_context.mission}
    COMPANY STATE: {org_context.state_summary}
    
    Weigh both positions against the mission. Choose the path that
    best serves the mission's achievement. Explain your reasoning.
    The losing executive's concerns should be acknowledged and, where
    possible, partially addressed in the resolution.
    """
    return llm_call(prompt)
```

#### 8. Failure Modes
- **Escalation overload**: every minor disagreement escalates to SALAAR. Guard: 2-round direct resolution requirement before escalation.
- **Executive siloing**: departments stop communicating, organization fragments. Guard: cross-functional team auto-convening for multi-department decisions.
- **Decision paralysis**: too many approvals required. Guard: default approval thresholds. Only high-impact decisions require multi-executive sign-off.

### Chapter 21: Division & Department Generator

#### 1. Purpose
To transform the organization blueprint (Ch.19) into concrete, operational divisions and departments — each with a clear mandate, KPIs, resource allocation, and executive team. The Generator takes the "what" from the Organization Engine and produces the "how" — the actual operational units that will execute.

#### 2. Philosophy
Divisions and departments are not static org-chart boxes. They are living operational units with: purpose (why they exist), objectives (what success looks like this quarter), resources (budget, headcount, tools), processes (how work gets done), and interfaces (how they interact with other units). The Generator creates these specifications. As the company evolves, departments split (a Marketing department that grows too large splits into Performance, Brand, Content), merge (two underperforming departments combine), or retire (a department whose function is no longer needed).

#### 3. Principles
- **DG1 — Purpose-first.** Every department has a one-sentence purpose statement that traces to the mission.
- **DG2 — Right-sized.** Departments are sized to the company stage. No 10-person departments in a 5-person company.
- **DG3 — Autonomous within mandate.** Departments have decision rights within their domain. They don't escalate operational decisions.
- **DG4 — Accountable for outcomes.** Every department has 3-5 measurable KPIs. Performance is tracked weekly.
- **DG5 — Evolvable.** Departments can split, merge, or retire. The Generator handles all three.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Organization Blueprint → Department Specification Generator
    → For each department: purpose, objectives, KPIs, budget, headcount,
      required executive roles, interfaces with other departments
    → Founder review → Activation
    → Weekly health check (Ch.48)
    → Quarterly evolution check (Ch.50)
```

#### 5. Data Model
```
departments (embedded in org.organization.divisions[].departments[]):
{
    id, name, function, purpose_statement,
    quarterly_objectives: [{objective, kpis: [{name, target, current}]}],
    budget: {total_inr, allocated_inr, spent_inr, burn_rate},
    headcount: {executives: int, specialists: int, temporary: int},
    interfaces: [{other_department_id, type: input|output|collaboration,
                  protocol: how_they_work_together}],

    created_at, evolved_at, performance_score: 0-100
}
```

### Chapter 22: Executive DNA

#### 1. Purpose
To define the complete specification of an executive — the data structure, behavioral model, and lifecycle rules that make an executive more than a prompt. Executive DNA is what transforms a generic LLM call into a persistent, accountable, learning organizational entity.

#### 2. Philosophy
An executive without DNA is just a system prompt with a title. An executive WITH DNA has: identity (who they are in the organization), authority (what they can decide), accountability (what they're measured on), memory (what they've learned), relationships (who they work with), and trajectory (how they grow). The DNA is the executable specification. It is what allows an executive to be instantiated, paused, promoted, demoted, merged, split, archived, or restored — all without losing their accumulated knowledge.

#### 3. Principles
- **ED1 — Complete specification.** Every executive has a complete DNA record. No implicit behavior.
- **ED2 — Portable knowledge.** Executive knowledge is stored in the DNA, not in prompt context. An executive can be re-instantiated from DNA alone.
- **ED3 — Measurable performance.** Every executive has KPIs. Performance history is part of the DNA.
- **ED4 — Learnable.** DNA updates based on outcomes. An executive that led 50 successful projects has different DNA than a freshly instantiated one.
- **ED5 — Transferable.** When an executive is promoted or moved to a different department, their accumulated knowledge transfers with them.

#### 4. Architecture
Executive DNA = static specification + dynamic state:

**Static (defined at creation, changes slowly):**
- Role, department, reporting line
- Mission (what they're accountable for)
- Authority (decision rights, spending limits, escalation rules)
- Communication style, risk appetite
- Domain knowledge injected at creation

### Chapter 23: Organization Hierarchy & Communication

#### 1. Purpose
To define how information flows through the executive organization — who talks to whom, how decisions are communicated, how knowledge spreads, and how the organization avoids both information silos and information overload.

#### 2. Philosophy
In human organizations, information flow is the hardest problem. The same is true for AI executive organizations. Too little communication → departments operate in ignorance of each other. Too much → every executive is CC'd on everything, creating noise that drowns signal. The communication architecture must balance: vertical communication (up and down the hierarchy for decisions and escalations), horizontal communication (across departments for collaboration), broadcast communication (organization-wide announcements that affect everyone), and selective communication (targeted information based on relevance).

#### 3. Principles
- **HC1 — Need-to-know by default.** Executives receive information relevant to their department's domain and active projects. Not everything.
- **HC2 — Decision notifications are targeted.** When a decision is made, notify executives whose departments are affected, not the entire org.
- **HC3 — Escalation is visible.** When an executive escalates, their superior AND any other executives affected by the underlying issue are notified.
- **HC4 — Learning is broadcast.** When an executive learns something that could benefit other departments, it's shared.
- **HC5 — Silence is consent with deadline.** If an executive is asked for input and doesn't respond within SLA, their silence is treated as no objection (with explicit opt-out for high-impact decisions).

#### 4. Architecture
```
Communication Types:
    1. Directive (top-down): "Execute this plan" — from superior to subordinate
    2. Consultation (horizontal): "How would this affect your department?" — peer to peer
    3. Escalation (bottom-up): "I cannot resolve this within my authority" — subordinate to superior
    4. Broadcast (one-to-many): "Company priority changed to X" — SALAAR to all
    5. Learning Share (peer-to-many): "We tried X and here's what happened" — any executive to relevant peers
    
Message Protocol:
    - Structured JSON, not free-text
    - Priority + deadline + expected response type
    - Threaded (replies link to original)
    - Immutable (messages not edited, only replied to)
```

See Chapter 20 (Executive Organization Architecture) for executive_messages and executive_decisions collections.

### Chapter 24: Internal Economy Engine

#### 1. Purpose
To simulate the resource allocation dynamics of a real company — where departments compete for budget, resources, and priority, and SALAAR (as CEO) allocates based on strategic value, not equal distribution. The Internal Economy Engine prevents every department from saying "I need more" by making the trade-offs explicit.

#### 2. Philosophy
In a real company, the CEO doesn't give every department what they ask for. Finance says "cut costs." Marketing says "increase spend." Engineering says "we need more time." The CEO weighs these against the mission and allocates accordingly. The Internal Economy Engine simulates this dynamic. Departments submit resource requests with justification. SALAAR evaluates against mission, current priorities, past performance, and opportunity cost. Some requests are granted. Some are denied with reasoning. Some are partially granted with conditions. The process is transparent — every executive sees why resources were allocated as they were.

#### 3. Principles
- **IE1 — Mission-weighted allocation.** Resources flow to departments whose work most directly serves the current mission priorities.
- **IE2 — Performance-weighted.** Departments with stronger track records of converting resources into outcomes receive preference.
- **IE3 — Zero-based by default.** Every allocation cycle, departments justify their budget from zero. No automatic renewals.
- **IE4 — Trade-offs are explicit.** When Marketing gets more budget, Engineering's budget may decrease. The reasoning is visible to both.
- **IE5 — Founder approves significant reallocations.** Routine adjustments are autonomous. Shifts >20% of total budget require founder approval.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Weekly/Quarterly Allocation Cycle:
    1. SALAAR sets strategic priorities for the period
    2. Departments submit resource requests with justification
    3. SALAAR evaluates each against:
        - Strategic alignment (how directly does this serve current priorities?)
        - Past ROI (what did this department achieve with last period's resources?)
        - Opportunity cost (what could the same resources achieve elsewhere?)
        - Risk (what happens if we underfund this department?)
    4. SALAAR produces allocation proposal
    5. Founder reviews (if shifts > threshold)
    6. Allocation enacted → budgets updated → executives notified
```

#### 5. Data Model
```
resource_requests: {
    id, department_id, executive_id, period_start, period_end,
    requested_budget_inr, requested_headcount, justification,
    expected_outcomes: [{description, metric, target, confidence}],
    status: submitted|under_review|approved|denied|partially_approved,
    allocated_budget_inr, allocated_headcount, salaar_reasoning,
    founder_modified: bool, created_at, resolved_at
}
```

#### 6. Decision Logic
**SALAAR's allocation algorithm:**
```
function allocate_resources(departments, requests, total_budget, priorities):
    scored = []
    for req in requests:
        dept = find_department(departments, req.department_id)
        alignment_score = calculate_alignment(req, priorities)
        past_roi = dept.performance.last_quarter_outcomes / dept.performance.last_quarter_budget
        risk_score = calculate_risk_of_underfunding(dept)
        score = (alignment_score * 0.4) + (past_roi * 0.3) + (risk_score * 0.3)
        scored.append({req, score})
    
    scored.sort(by=score, descending)
    allocated = []
    remaining_budget = total_budget
    for item in scored:
        if remaining_budget >= item.req.requested_budget_inr:
            allocate_full(item)
        elif remaining_budget > item.req.requested_budget_inr * 0.5:
            allocate_partial(item, remaining_budget)
        else:
            deny_with_reason(item, "Insufficient budget for minimum viable allocation")
    return allocated
```

### Chapter 25: Company Culture Engine

#### 1. Purpose
To define, instantiate, and enforce a company culture — the set of values, decision principles, and behavioral norms that guide how the executive organization operates. Culture is not a slogan on a wall. It is the algorithm that governs how executives make decisions when the rules are ambiguous.

#### 2. Philosophy
Every company has a culture, whether intentionally designed or not. Amazon's is customer obsession. Apple's is product perfection. Toyota's is continuous improvement. SpaceX's is extreme execution speed. The Company Culture Engine generates a culture that fits the founder's values and the company's mission, then ensures every executive decision is consistent with that culture. When an executive faces a judgment call with no clear rule, culture provides the tiebreaker.

#### 3. Principles
- **CE1 — Mission-aligned.** Culture serves the mission. A mission to "build India's most trusted solar brand" requires a culture of quality and transparency, not "move fast and break things."
- **CE2 — Founder-reflective.** Culture reflects the founder's values, as expressed in their Decision DNA (Ch.11).
- **CE3 — Enforced, not displayed.** Culture is embedded in executive decision prompts. Executives don't "remember the culture." They think within it.
- **CE4 — Observable.** Culture violations are detectable. If an executive makes a decision inconsistent with stated culture, it's flagged.
- **CE5 — Evolutionary.** Culture evolves as the company grows. A startup's "bias for action" culture may evolve into a scale-up's "disciplined execution" culture.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Culture Definition: 5-7 cultural principles, each with:
    - Principle statement (e.g., "Customer outcomes over internal convenience")
    - Decision heuristic (how this principle guides choices)
    - Anti-pattern (what violating this principle looks like)
    - Examples (3-5 scenarios showing the principle in action)

Culture Injection: cultural principles embedded in every executive's system prompt.
    Not as an appendix. As part of the core decision-making instruction.

Culture Monitoring: weekly scan of executive decisions for culture violations.
    Flagged decisions reviewed by SALAAR.
    Repeated violations → executive coaching or restructuring.
```

#### 5. Data Model
```

    violation_threshold: int  // how many violations before action
}

culture_violations: {

    created_at
}
```

### Chapter 26: Organization Memory

#### 1. Purpose
To define how the executive organization remembers — not just individual executive memory (Ch.31), but the collective memory of the organization itself. What the organization has learned, what processes work, what vendors are reliable, what strategies succeeded or failed. Organization Memory is what makes a 6-month-old AI executive organization more capable than a freshly generated one.

#### 2. Philosophy
Individual memory is what one executive knows. Organization Memory is what the company knows — even if the specific executives who learned it are no longer active. When a Marketing executive runs 50 campaigns and is then archived (company pivots), their campaign knowledge doesn't disappear. It becomes part of Organization Memory. The next Marketing executive inherits it. Organization Memory is the compound knowledge of the entire organization's operational history.

#### 3. Principles
- **OM1 — Survives executive lifecycle.** Knowledge outlives the executives who acquired it.
- **OM2 — Organized by domain.** Marketing knowledge, Engineering knowledge, Sales knowledge — stored separately, retrievable by domain.
- **OM3 — Confidence-weighted.** Organization memories carry confidence based on how many times a pattern was observed.
- **OM4 — Decays if unused.** Knowledge not accessed for 12 months is archived (not deleted — available if needed).
- **OM5 — Cross-pollinates.** When a pattern in one department is relevant to another, it's shared (via broadcast communication, Ch.23).

#### 4. Architecture
```
Sources of Organization Memory:
    - Executive DNA dumps (when an executive is archived, their knowledge is extracted)
    - Autopsy Engine outputs (Ch.18: what worked, what didn't, root causes)
    - Department performance data (which strategies produced which outcomes)
    - Cross-department pattern detection (Ch.47 Optimization Engine)

Storage:
    organization_memory collection, organized by domain and tagged with:
    - source (which executive/department/autopsy produced this)
    - confidence (how many times observed)
    - recency (when last accessed)
    - applicable_context (when this knowledge is relevant)

Retrieval:
    - Embedding-based similarity search (same infrastructure as doc retrieval, Ch.14)
    - Injected into new executive instantiation as initial knowledge
    - Injected into executive decisions as "what we know about this"
```

#### 5. Data Model
```

### Chapter 27: Executive Lifecycle

#### 1. Purpose
To define the complete lifecycle of an executive — from creation through operation to archival or promotion. This replaces the v1.0 "Agent Lifecycle" concept. Executives are not temporary agents spawned per-task. They are persistent organizational entities with state, memory, relationships, and careers.

#### 2. Philosophy
An executive's lifecycle mirrors a human executive's career: they are hired (instantiated) for a specific role, they have a probation period where their decisions are more closely reviewed, they gain autonomy as they demonstrate competence, they may be promoted to larger roles, they may be moved to different departments, they may be put on performance review if their outcomes decline, and they may eventually be archived (if their role is no longer needed) with their knowledge preserved. This lifecycle creates accountability — executives know their performance is tracked and their continued existence depends on it.

#### 3. Principles
- **EL1 — Probation on creation.** New executives have limited autonomous decision rights for the first 2 weeks (or 20 decisions). SALAAR reviews their decisions.
- **EL2 — Performance-based autonomy.** As an executive demonstrates good outcomes, their autonomous decision rights expand.
- **EL3 — Promotion with expanded authority.** An executive promoted to a larger role inherits their accumulated knowledge but receives expanded authority and new KPIs.
- **EL4 — Graceful archival.** When a department is dissolved or restructured, executives are archived — their DNA and knowledge preserved, ready to be restored if needed.
- **EL5 — No silent failure.** An executive whose performance drops below threshold is flagged, not silently degraded. The issue is surfaced to their superior.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Lifecycle States:
    instantiation → probation → active → (promoted | under_review | archived)
    
    instantiation: DNA assembled, knowledge injected, system prompt built
    probation: active but decisions reviewed by SALAAR, authority limited
    active: full authority within defined scope, independent operation
    promoted: moved to larger role, DNA updated, new authority, preserved knowledge
    under_review: performance below threshold, decisions reviewed, improvement plan
    archived: role no longer needed, DNA + knowledge preserved, can be restored
    merged: combined with another executive (department merge), knowledge transferred
```

#### 5. Data Model
See executive DNA (Ch.22) for the full executive data model. Lifecycle fields:
```
executive.lifecycle: {
    status, created_at, probation_ends_at,
    promoted_at, promoted_from_role,
    under_review_since, improvement_plan,
    archived_at, archive_reason, restorable: bool,
    merged_into_executive_id, merged_at
}
```

#### 6. Decision Logic
**When to promote:** executive consistently exceeds KPIs for 8+ weeks, has handled increasing complexity, and the organization has a need at a higher level.
**When to review:** executive's 4-week rolling average KPI score drops below threshold, or 3+ culture violations in a quarter, or 2+ escalations of their decisions overturned.
**When to archive:** department dissolved, company pivot makes role unnecessary, or executive performance doesn't improve after review period.

The Risk Planning engine makes risks explicit: what could go wrong, how likely is it, what's the impact, what's the mitigation, what's the trigger that tells us it's happening, what's the contingency if it does. Risks are monitored continuously, not reviewed quarterly. When a risk trigger fires, the responsible executive is immediately notified.

#### 3. Principles
- **RP1 — Every project has a risk register.** Mandatory for projects above a configurable impact threshold.
- **RP2 — Risks have owners.** Every identified risk is assigned to an executive accountable for monitoring it.
- **RP3 — Triggers, not just descriptions.** Every risk defines observable triggers — specific events or metric changes that indicate the risk is materializing.
- **RP4 — Contingencies, not just mitigations.** Mitigation reduces likelihood. Contingency is what we do if it happens anyway.
- **RP5 — Portfolio view.** SALAAR sees aggregate risk across all projects. Correlation risks identified (two projects depending on same vendor).

### Chapter 41: Dependency Engine

### Chapter 43: Simulation Engine

#### 1. Purpose
To answer "what if" questions about the organization: "What happens if we hire 10 more salespeople?", "What if we delay the product launch by 3 months?", "What if we reallocate 20% of marketing budget to engineering?" The Simulation Engine models organizational changes before they're implemented.

#### 2. Philosophy
The best way to avoid bad decisions is to simulate them first. The Simulation Engine takes a proposed change, models its effects through the organization (using the digital twin, historical performance data, dependency graph, and resource model), and produces a predicted outcome. The simulation is not a guarantee — it's a structured thought experiment that surfaces second-order effects the proposer may not have considered.

#### 3. Principles
- **SM1 — Evidence-based simulation.** Models use actual historical data from this organization, not generic assumptions.
- **SM2 — Multiple scenarios.** Best case, worst case, most likely. With confidence bands.
- **SM3 — Second-order effects surfaced.** "Adding 10 salespeople" → "Operations capacity must increase 30%" → "Operations budget insufficient" → "Hiring Operations executives required."
- **SM4 — Assumptions explicit.** Every simulation states its assumptions. If an assumption is wrong, the simulation is invalid.

# LAYER 7: VERIFICATION RUNTIME

Nothing important is trusted automatically. Every outcome is verified at the highest confidence level available.

### Chapter 44: Verification Engine

#### 1. Purpose
To verify that executed actions produced their intended results. The Verification Engine closes the loop between "I did it" and "it worked." It answers: did the action actually happen, did it produce the expected outcome, and if not, why not?

#### 2. Philosophy
Self-report is the weakest form of verification. The Verification Engine layers verification methods by confidence:
- **Self-report** (confidence: 0.3): member or executive marks task done.
- **AI review** (confidence: 0.6): AI analyzes proof files, checks against expected output.
- **System evidence** (confidence: 0.8): automated data validation (CRM shows deal closed, bank shows payment received).
- **External verification** (confidence: 0.95): third-party confirmation (auditor, customer confirmation, regulatory filing).
- **Outcome verification** (confidence: 0.9): the predicted outcome actually materialized (revenue increased, churn decreased).

The engine starts with what's available and improves over time as integrations expand.

#### 3. Principles
- **VE1 — Layered verification.** Multiple methods with confidence scores. Higher confidence overrides lower.
- **VE2 — Proof required for significant actions.** Actions above configurable impact threshold require proof, not just self-report.
- **VE3 — Verification feeds learning loop.** Verified results → updated calibration → improved future predictions.
- **VE4 — Graceful degradation.** If automated verification unavailable, fall back to self-report + AI review.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Task Marked Done → Verification Router:
    - Low impact task → self-report sufficient
    - Medium impact → AI review + self-report
    - High impact → system evidence required + AI review
    - Critical impact → external verification required + all of above
→ Verification Outcome: verified | flagged | failed
→ Flagged: surfaced to accountable executive for manual review
→ Failed: task re-opened, assigned back with feedback
```

#### 5. Data Model
```
verification_records: {
    id, task_id, project_id, org_id,
    methods: [{type, confidence, evidence, reviewed_by, at}],
    overall_confidence: float,
    status: verified|flagged|failed,
    flagged_reason, resolved_by, resolved_at
}
```

### Chapter 45: Outcome Verification

#### 1. Purpose
To verify that the *predicted outcome* of a decision actually materialized. This is distinct from task verification (Ch.44) — a task can be "done" but the outcome can still fail to materialize. Outcome Verification is the honest feedback loop.

#### 2. Philosophy
"We launched the campaign" is task verification. "Revenue increased 15%" is outcome verification. The latter is what actually matters. Outcome Verification runs when a decision's review_at date arrives. It checks: did the predicted metric move? By how much? In the predicted direction? With what confidence? The result feeds the Autopsy Engine (Ch.18) and updates the organization's calibration curve.

#### 3. Principles
- **OV1 — Metric-gated.** Outcome verification requires a measurable metric. Predictions without measurable metrics are flagged as unverifiable.
- **OV2 — Automatic where possible.** If the metric is tracked in the digital twin (Ch.12), verification is automatic.
- **OV3 — Time-aware.** Some outcomes take 7 days, some take 90. Verification respects the review_after_days.
- **OV4 — Honest about ambiguity.** If external factors could explain the outcome (market shift, competitor action), that's noted — not attributed to the decision.

### Chapter 46: Recovery & Rollback

#### 1. Purpose
To define how the organization recovers from execution failures, wrong decisions, and unintended consequences. Not everything goes according to plan. Recovery & Rollback ensures the organization can gracefully handle failure.

#### 2. Philosophy
Failure is inevitable. What matters is: how quickly is it detected, how effectively is it contained, how thoroughly is it learned from, and how smoothly is normal operation restored. Recovery & Rollback defines: detection (when does the system know something went wrong?), containment (how do we prevent the failure from cascading?), rollback (how do we undo what was done?), analysis (what caused it? — feeds Autopsy ch.18), and prevention (what changes prevent recurrence?).

#### 3. Principles

- **RR2 — Rollback capability for critical actions.** Actions with irreversible consequences require an explicit rollback plan before execution.
- **RR3 — Failure is contained, not cascaded.** A failure in Department A does not halt Department B's work.
- **RR4 — Every recovery is a learning event.** Recovery feeds the Autopsy Engine. The organization gets better at handling failure.

### Chapter 50: Organization Evolution

#### 1. Purpose
To manage the structural evolution of the executive organization as the company grows, pivots, or enters new markets. The organization that serves a 5-person startup is not the organization that serves a 500-person enterprise. Evolution is automatic, proposed, and founder-approved.

#### 2. Philosophy
Companies don't reorganize often enough. They cling to structures that made sense 2 years ago. The Organization Evolution engine runs quarterly: given the current company stage, size, strategy, and performance data, what organizational changes would improve outcome-achievement? Proposals include: split a department that's grown too large, merge two underperforming departments, create a new division for a new market, archive an executive role that's no longer needed, promote an executive who's operating above their level, adjust reporting lines that create bottlenecks.

#### 3. Principles
- **OE1 — Quarterly evolution review.** Automatic. Not waiting for a crisis.
- **OE2 — Evidence-driven proposals.** Every change proposal backed by performance data.
- **OE3 — Founder-approved.** Evolution proposals are recommendations, not auto-executed.
- **OE4 — Knowledge-preserving.** Structural changes preserve all executive and organizational memory.
- **OE5 — Staged rollout.** Major reorganizations are phased, not big-bang.

#### 4. Architecture
```
Quarterly Evolution Review:
    1. Analyze: org health trends, performance data, growth trajectory, strategy changes
    2. Generate: evolution proposals (splits, merges, promotions, archival, new divisions)
    3. Simulate: for each proposal, model expected impact using Simulation Engine (Ch.43)
    4. Rank: proposals by expected outcome improvement
    5. Present: top proposals to founder with evidence and simulation results
    6. Execute: founder-approved changes → phased rollout → monitor outcomes
```

```

### Chapter 52: Institutional Memory Engine

#### 1. Purpose
To ensure that the organization's accumulated knowledge survives structural changes, executive archival, and company pivots. Institutional Memory is the organization's "long-term memory" — what the company knows regardless of who currently works there.

#### 2. Philosophy
Human organizations lose knowledge when people leave. SmartDecigen's organization must not. Institutional Memory stores: every significant decision and its outcome, every strategy that was tried and what happened, every process that was developed and refined, every vendor relationship and its history, every customer insight, every technical architecture decision and its rationale. This memory is queryable by any current executive. When a new executive is born, they're injected with relevant institutional memory for their domain. The organization doesn't repeat mistakes because it literally remembers making them.

#### 3. Principles
- **IM1 — Permanent by default.** Institutional memories are never deleted, only archived.
- **IM2 — Domain-organized.** Financial memory, technical memory, market memory, operational memory — separate, cross-referenced.
- **IM3 — Context-rich.** Every memory includes: what happened, when, why, what we learned, what we'd do differently.
- **IM4 — Queryable.** Executives can query institutional memory: "What happened the last time we tried enterprise sales?"
- **IM5 — Confidence-tagged.** Memories based on single events have lower confidence than patterns observed across multiple events.

### Appendix D: Constitution Amendment Process

1. **Proposal**: Identify the chapter, the change, the evidence, and the trade-off.
2. **5-Test Review**: Apply Vision, Organization, Consistency, Scalability, and Model Independence tests.
3. **Impact Analysis**: Which other chapters are affected? Are they still consistent?
4. **Approval**:
   - Foundation chapters (1-8): Unanimous founding team.
   - Intelligence chapters (9-18): Majority founding team + SALAAR consistency check.
   - Organization & Runtime chapters (19-52): Majority founding team.
   - Platform chapters (53-60): Engineering lead + majority founding team.
5. **Publication**: Amended constitution published. All engineers notified. Old version archived.
6. **Propagation**: Affected subsystems updated within one sprint to align with amendment.
